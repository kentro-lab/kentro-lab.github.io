This is how work moves through a system we build

One invoice arrives at 07:42. Follow it to the end of the month.

Kentro AI Labs is an AI-engineering firm. We take small and mid-sized businesses from strategy through deployment, and then operate what we build. Rather than describe that, this page runs the example. A single supplier invoice lands in an inbox below. Scroll, and watch it move through the workflow we would build for it — read, checked, decided on by a person, and posted — with a note in the margin at every step for anyone who wants the engineering underneath.

An illustrative walkthrough

This is the shape of what we build — not a specific client system. The supplier, the numbers, and the timestamps are invented scenario detail, chosen to be realistic. We name no clients and show no real customer data.

07:42Arrives
Station one

It arrives in the inbox.

An email lands with a PDF attached. Nobody has touched it yet. This is the moment where, in most businesses, someone opens the attachment and starts retyping it by hand into the accounting system. We start the clock here.

07:43Read
Station two

It gets read.

The system reads the document the way a person would — but it also records how sure it is about each field it pulls out. The highlighted values below were extracted automatically; the small blue mark next to each is the system's confidence. Anything below the threshold is held back rather than trusted.

Engineering note ›

Extraction is OCR plus a vision-language model, forced to emit a typed schema. Each field carries a calibrated confidence, and the shape is validated before anything downstream sees it — totals must reconcile, dates must parse, the PO must exist.

Fields under the threshold (here the 0.71 on delivery) never post silently. They route to review. We tune the threshold per field against a labelled set, because a wrong number that looks confident is the expensive failure.

07:43Checked
Station three

It gets checked, and something does not match.

Before any judgment call, the system runs plain, deterministic checks: does the invoice match the purchase order, and does each line agree with what this vendor has billed before? Two lines reconcile cleanly. One does not — the anchor-bolt price is higher than the agreed PO rate — so the system raises a flag instead of guessing.

Engineering note ›

The checks that decide whether to flag are deterministic code, not the model: PO lookup, line-level price tolerance, vendor-history comparison, duplicate detection. The model reads; rules decide.

Every check writes to an append-only log — inputs, the rule that fired, the outcome. When someone asks months later why this invoice was held, the answer is a record, not a reconstruction.

07:44Decided
Station four

A person decides.

The exception does not go into a void — it goes to a named role with a one-line explanation and a clear choice. The system knows what it does not know, and a person stays accountable for the call. That is the whole point: automation earns trust by handing off the cases it should not settle alone.

Routed to your accounts-payable lead · INV-4419

Anchor bolt set, M12 is billed at 3.25, twelve percent over the PO rate of 2.90. Everything else on the invoice matched. Approve to post as billed, or reject to query the supplier.

Approved by the AP lead at 07:51 — releasing to post.
Engineering note ›

Human-in-the-loop is a threshold, not a philosophy. Below a per-check confidence we defined with you, the item waits for a person; above it, the system posts straight through. Roughly nineteen in twenty clear without a human in a well-tuned pipeline.

The decision, the person, and the timestamp are written to the audit trail alongside the machine's reasoning — so an approval is attributable and reversible.

07:51Posted
Station five

It is posted, into the system you already use.

With the approval recorded, the entry is written into the accounting system your team already runs. No new tool to learn, no export-and-paste. The invoice that arrived at 07:42 is reconciled and filed nine minutes later, and the only human moment was the one that actually needed judgment.

Why there is no new tool

We connect to and extend what you already run. The system reads from and writes to your existing ERP or accounting software through its own interfaces, so your team keeps working where they always have — the automation happens behind the screens they already know.

Engineering note ›

Integration is through the accounting or ERP system's own API — Xero, QuickBooks, NetSuite, Sage, or an internal ledger — with idempotency keys so a retry never double-posts.

The pipeline runs on your cloud of choice (GCP or AWS). Runs are traced end to end and cost-metered per invoice, so the unit economics are visible from day one rather than at the first surprising bill.

MonthEnd
Station six

At the end of the month, a quiet report.

One invoice became a habit. The system ran every day, watched itself, and escalated only what a person should see. This is what production actually means: running, observed, and boring in the best possible way. A short summary lands each month — no dashboard to check, no fire to fight.

Engineering note ›

The report is a view over the same evaluation and observability data that runs continuously: accuracy against a held-out set, escalation rate, cost per run, drift alerts.

If a model or a supplier's format shifts and quality slips, the eval catches it before a person does, and a versioned rollback is one switch — recovery is a decision, not an incident.

Other things that take this journey

The invoice is one example. The pattern is the product.

Swap the invoice for anything that arrives as unstructured work and needs to be read, checked, decided on, and acted upon. The same spine — extract, validate, escalate the hard cases, act inside your systems, watch it in production — carries each of them.

A customer email follows the same path when it needs classifying, drafting a reply, and logging against the right account. A support ticket does when it must be triaged, grounded in your documentation, and either answered or routed to a person. A contract does when its key terms and dates must be extracted, checked against your standard positions, and filed. A stack of receipts does when it becomes reconciled expense lines. These map onto the three areas of work we take on, described here in prose rather than a grid, because the point is that they are one method applied, not three separate products.

  • A customer email→ chatbots and agentic workflows
  • A support ticket→ chatbots and agentic workflows
  • A contract→ document processing
  • A stack of receipts→ document processing
  • Your team's own tools→ AI enablement for your team

The interactive check needs JavaScript. Here are the eight lenses it scores you on — read them, judge yourself honestly on each, then tell us where you landed.

  1. DataDo the records this task depends on already live in a system that can be searched or exported, rather than only in inboxes and people's heads?
  2. Workflow fitHave you identified a specific, repeatable task with a clear definition of a correct result, rather than a general wish to use AI somewhere?
  3. IntegrationDo the tools involved offer an API, an export, or another reliable way for a system to read from and write back to them?
  4. EvaluationCould you gather a set of examples with known correct answers to test the system's quality before and after it goes live?
  5. OversightIs it clear who reviews the AI's output and what happens when it is unsure, before it touches a real decision?
  6. Cost modelDo you have a rough sense of what one automated run is worth to you and roughly what it should be allowed to cost?
  7. SecurityDo you know which of the data involved is sensitive and what your privacy or contractual rules require before it leaves your systems?
  8. Team adoptionAre the people whose daily work would change involved early and open to a new way of doing the task?
