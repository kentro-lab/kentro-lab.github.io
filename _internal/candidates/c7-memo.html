§ 1

The problem we keep seeing

Most AI initiatives stall in the same place: the gap between a demo that convinces a room and a system anyone can rely on. A working demonstration takes an afternoon. A system that runs every day — grounded in your data, connected to your tools, watched, measured, and safe to change — takes engineering.2 That gap is where promising projects quietly die, and it is the only part of this work worth paying for.

We recognize a handful of situations again and again. “We’re drowning in paperwork” — a capable person spends hours retyping invoices, receipts, and forms into a system that could read them. “We know AI could help, but not where to start” — every vendor pitch sounds the same and none begins with your business. “We tried a pilot and it stalled” — a demo worked in a meeting, everyone was excited, and then it never became part of the day, because there was no connection to live data, no handling of the messy cases, and no way to tell when it was wrong.3

None of these are model problems. They are engineering and judgment problems: which task is worth automating, whether the data is there, how the result reaches the people who need it, and how you know it is still correct next month. That is the work this memo is about.

§ 2

What we build

Our work falls into three areas. Each is a running system, not a slide, and each is grounded in your own data and connected to the tools you already use.

Document processing

We pull structured, verified data out of the paperwork a business runs on, with a confidence score on every field and a human-review path for anything the system is unsure about. A typical build reads incoming invoices and receipts, extracts the fields you actually use, reconciles them against your existing records, and writes the result straight into your accounting or ERP system4 — so the outcome is fewer keystrokes every morning, not a clever demonstration.

Assistants and agentic workflows

We build assistants and multi-step agents that answer from your own knowledge and act inside your systems. Answers are traceable to a source; consequential actions pass an evaluation gate or a person before they run.5 Named examples: a support assistant grounded in your documentation and past tickets, an internal agent that looks up records and drafts the reply, or a workflow that carries a routine multi-step task from start to finish across connected tools.

Team enablement

We help the people whose work changes use these tools well and safely — practical training on real tasks from your week, prompt patterns and review checklists for your workflows, and plain guidelines on what is safe to share. Not a one-off workshop, but habits and guardrails that hold after we leave.

Underneath all three is ordinary software engineering done well. Our team brings more than fifteen years across full-stack and backend development, data-intensive systems, cloud infrastructure on Google Cloud and AWS, and machine-learning platforms.6 That substrate is what separates a promising demo from something you can run on Monday morning.

§ 3

How we work

A prototype is a question; production is the answer, and it has a checklist. These are the four practices we hold to on every build, not extras we quote separately.

  1. We evaluate before launch, and keep evaluating.

    We build a representative test set from your real cases and gate releases against it.7 A change that improves one thing and quietly breaks another should fail the gate, not the customer.

  2. Every run is traceable.

    Inputs, tool calls, model outputs, and costs are logged so you can answer “why did it do that?” after the fact.8 Operating a system you cannot see into is guessing.

  3. There is a safe path back.

    Prompts, models, and tools are versioned, and changes ship behind a switch we can reverse. When something regresses — and eventually something will — recovery is a decision, not an incident.

  4. You own what we build.

    The code, the prompts, and the data are yours, documented for a team that is not us. We would rather build something you can run than something that needs us to keep breathing.

§ 4

What an engagement looks like, and what it costs to find out

An engagement moves through up to three shapes. You always know what you are buying before we begin, you can stop after any stage, and the discovery call that precedes all of it is free.

Assess
Fixed fee · about two weeks

We look at one candidate workflow, your data, and your systems, then tell you plainly whether to build it — and if so, exactly what.

  • A written findings document, not a slide deck
  • A clear build-or-do-not-build recommendation
  • A scoped design and cost estimate for the Build, if warranted
  • The data and integration risks we found, named honestly

You can stop here with a clear answer and owe nothing further.

Build
Fixed scope · fixed price

We build the system defined in the Assess to a scope and price agreed in writing, and deploy it into your workflow rather than handing over a prototype.9

  • A working system deployed in your environment
  • Evaluation tests and a human-review path built in
  • Source code, prompts, and configuration, handed to you
  • Documentation your team can maintain from

You can stop here and run it yourself; the handover is part of the work.

Operate
Monthly retainer · optional

Models drift, data changes, and edge cases surface in the real world. If you want it, we monitor the system, keep it accurate, and improve it as your needs move.10

  • Monitoring of accuracy, cost, and failures in production
  • Regular tuning as your data and volumes shift
  • A named point of contact and a response commitment
  • No lock-in — take operations in-house whenever you choose

Prices are fixed and agreed in writing before each stage. If the scope changes, we agree the change with you first — never after the invoice.

§ 5

Whether this is worth doing at all

Sometimes the honest answer is no. Not every business needs AI yet, and some problems are cheaper to fix with a spreadsheet, a process change, or a plain piece of software. When that is what we find, we say so in the Assess findings and recommend against a build — and you will have paid a fixed fee for a clear answer rather than a stalled project. We would rather forgo an engagement than ship a system you cannot depend on.

These are the criteria we use to say no:

  • The data is not there. The records the task depends on live only in inboxes and people’s heads, and getting them into a usable form is the real first project.
  • The workflow is not stable. The task changes shape week to week, so there is no fixed definition of a correct result to build against.
  • A simpler tool would do. The problem is a rules engine, an export, or a form — not a model — and calling it AI would only add cost and risk.
  • The value does not clear the cost. The volume is too low, or the outcome too cheap, for automation to pay for itself once you count the cost per run.

A stalled pilot is more expensive than an honest no. Hearing which of these applies to you — early, in writing — is often the most valuable thing an Assess produces.

A self-check

These are the eight questions we would ask on the first call — the lenses that decide whether an AI system survives real use rather than merely demonstrating well. Score yourself first if you like. It is a self-assessment, not an audit: it reflects your own answers, stores nothing, and sends nothing anywhere.

  1. 01 · DataDo the records this task depends on already live in a system that can be searched or exported, rather than only in inboxes and people’s heads?
  2. 02 · Workflow fitHave you identified a specific, repeatable task with a clear definition of a correct result, rather than a general wish to use AI somewhere?
  3. 03 · IntegrationDo the tools involved offer an API, an export, or another reliable way for a system to read from and write back to them?
  4. 04 · EvaluationCould you gather a set of examples with known correct answers to test the system’s quality before and after it goes live?
  5. 05 · OversightIs it clear who reviews the AI’s output and what happens when it is unsure, before it touches a real decision?
  6. 06 · Cost modelDo you have a rough sense of what one automated run is worth to you and roughly what it should be allowed to cost?
  7. 07 · SecurityDo you know which of the data involved is sensitive and what your privacy or contractual rules require before it leaves your systems?
  8. 08 · Team adoptionAre the people whose daily work would change involved early and open to a new way of doing the task?

Appendix B

Plain-English glossary

A few terms from this memo, each in one sentence. If we cannot explain a thing plainly, we have not understood it well enough.

Agentagent
A program that uses a language model to decide what to do next and then takes the action itself, rather than only producing text for a person to act on.
Agentic workflowworkflow
A task broken into steps that an agent carries out in order — looking things up, deciding, and acting across several tools — with checkpoints where a person can intervene.
Grounding / RAGretrieval
Answering from your own documents by retrieving the relevant passages first, so the reply is based on your facts and can be traced back to its source.
Evaluation seteval set
A collection of example cases with known correct answers, used to measure how good the system is before launch and to catch it getting worse afterward.
Human in the loopoversight
A deliberate point where a person reviews or approves the system’s output — usually when it is unsure or the decision carries real consequences.
Inference costcost / run
What it costs each time the model runs; because it scales with usage, we track it from the first release so the economics are known before volume makes them urgent.
Confidence thresholdthreshold
The score below which the system stops trusting itself and routes a case to a person instead of acting on it automatically.
