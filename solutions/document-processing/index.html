The problem

Every sub and supplier sends invoices a different way.

One emails a PDF. One texts a photo of a paper slip. One hands your foreman a crumpled carbon copy. Your office manager retypes each one into QuickBooks, matches it to the right purchase order and the right job, and chases down the ones that look wrong. It eats hours every week — and things still slip through.

"My office manager retypes each one into QuickBooks, matches it to the right PO and job, and chases down the ones that look wrong. It eats hours, and things still slip through — we've paid the wrong amount more than once."

Overbillings get caught late, or not at all. A wrong quantity, a rate that doesn't match the PO, a duplicate invoice for work already billed — by the time anyone notices, the check has cleared. The work is tedious, it's error-prone, and it's exactly the kind of work AI is good at.

How it works

Four stages, no data entry.

The system reads each invoice however it arrives, checks it against what you already agreed to, and posts the clean ones straight into your accounting — handing your admin only the ones that don't add up.

Stage one

It arrives

An invoice lands however it comes — an emailed PDF, a photo, a scan, or a paper slip your office snaps a picture of.

Stage two

It reads and extracts

The AI pulls out the vendor, the amount, and the line items — and notes how confident it is in each field.

Stage three

It checks

It matches the invoice against your purchase order and your job budget — quantities, rates, and totals, line by line.

Stage four

It posts, or it flags

Clean invoices post straight into QuickBooks. Anything that doesn't match goes to your admin to approve before a cent is paid.

Follow one invoice

Watch a single invoice travel the line.

Here is one invoice — from a fictional supplier, on a fictional job — moving through every station. The names and numbers are made up to show the shape of the work; a real build is fitted to your own vendors, POs, and chart of accounts.

1

8:12 a.m. — the invoice lands

Delta Supply Co. emails invoice #4471 for the Maple Street remodel: 42 sheets of 5/8" drywall and delivery, totalling $3,880. It arrives as a slightly crooked phone photo attached to an email — the kind your office manager would normally squint at and retype by hand.

Arrives
2

8:12 a.m. — it reads and extracts

The AI reads the photo and pulls the details out: vendor Delta Supply Co., invoice #4471, the Maple Street job, 42 sheets at $86 each, plus a $268 delivery line. It's confident on the vendor and totals, and it notes that the delivery charge sits on its own — worth a second look.

Reads and extracts
3

8:12 a.m. — it checks against your PO and budget

It finds purchase order PO-Maple-019, which authorized 40 sheets at $86 and no separate delivery charge. The invoice bills 42 sheets and adds $268 for delivery. The vendor and rate match your PO; the quantity is two sheets over, and the delivery line was never agreed. Against the Maple Street budget, it's a $440 overage on the drywall line.

Checks
4

8:13 a.m. — it flags the exception for your admin

Because the invoice doesn't match the PO, it doesn't post. Instead it lands in your admin's queue with the mismatch spelled out plainly: "PO authorized 40 sheets at $86, no delivery; invoice bills 42 sheets plus $268 delivery — $440 over." Your admin sees exactly what to ask Delta Supply about, calls in, and learns two extra sheets went out to cover breakage and delivery was supposed to be waived. A person makes the call — the AI just did the finding.

A person stays in control
5

8:41 a.m. — a clean invoice posts itself

Delta reissues invoice #4471-R: 40 sheets at $86, delivery waived, $3,440 even. This time it matches PO-Maple-019 to the line. No one retypes anything — it posts straight into QuickBooks against the Maple Street job, coded to the right account, and your admin never had to touch it. The two-sheet overbilling and the stray delivery charge were caught before a check went out.
